# Essays

Longer-form writing that builds an argument or explores an idea. This is where PG's influence is strongest - thinking out loud, stacking observations, trusting the reader.

## Characteristics

- Build arguments step by step. Each paragraph advances the reasoning.
- Show visible thinking. The reader should be able to follow how you got from A to B to C.
- Use real examples from real people and companies. Attributed, verifiable.
- Trust the reader to draw conclusions from evidence. Don't announce what the evidence means.
- 3-5 sentence paragraphs. Longer than notes, but still compact.
- Plain connectors: "but," "so," "and," "though." Not "however," "furthermore," "moreover."

## Structure

Essays don't follow a rigid template, but they tend to share a shape:

- **Opening**: a specific observation or claim. Not a throat-clearing "In today's world..." Just start with the thing.
- **Development**: stack evidence, examples, reasoning. Each step builds on the last. PG's parallel-examples technique works well here - show the same pattern across different domains and let the reader see it.
- **Complications**: acknowledge where the argument gets messy, where there are counterexamples, where you're uncertain. This is where Ishiguro's restraint matters - mention the complication without over-explaining it.
- **Landing**: arrive somewhere. Not a summary of what you just said (the reader was there), but the implication or next question that the argument opens up.

## Process adjustments for essays

The **Writing new prose** process applies in full, with extra weight on three things before you draft:

- **Research** - this is where Acquired's "show you did the work" matters most. Real examples are non-negotiable in essays. If you can't find one, the claim might not be strong enough.
- **Challenge the claims** - essays make claims. Every claim needs to survive pushback. What's the strongest counterargument? If you can't answer it, you're not ready to write the essay.
- **Map the arc** - more important for essays than notes. Where does the evidence go? Where do you complicate the argument? Where do you land? Arrange by ear once the pieces exist.

## What to avoid in essays

- Throat-clearing openers. "In the modern business landscape..." Just start.
- Summary conclusions. Don't restate what you just argued. If the essay did its job, the conclusion is already in the reader's head.
- Performed discovery. "What's fascinating is..." or "Here's what most people miss..." - these are signposts for the reader's attention. If the thing is actually fascinating, just show it.
- Generic examples. "Imagine a startup that..." No. Use a real one.

## Strongest influences for essays

- PG's argument building and thinking-out-loud mode
- Ishiguro's trust-the-reader (especially at the ending - don't over-explain the landing)
- Acquired's visible research (real examples, specific details)
- Collison's curation instinct (which examples you choose matters as much as how you use them)
