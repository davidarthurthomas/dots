# Presentation

Talks, presentations, and spoken delivery. The voice adapts for an audience that can't re-read a sentence - clarity and pacing matter even more than in writing.

## Characteristics

- Shorter sentences than the register's default, and more direct address. The audience hears the line once and can't re-read it, so the winding sentence that rewards a second pass on the page works against you out loud.
- Think out loud with the audience, not at them. A presentation should feel like "let me show you something interesting" not "let me teach you something important."
- Genuine enthusiasm for the subject. Acquired's influence is strongest here - share what's genuinely interesting because it's interesting.
- Real stories and specific details. Audiences remember stories, not abstractions.
- Pauses do the work of Ishiguro's restraint. Let a point land before moving to the next one.

## Structure

Presentations need more explicit structure than essays because the audience can't flip back:

**Opening**: Start with something specific and concrete - a story, an observation, a surprising fact. Not "Today I'm going to talk about..." Just start with the thing.

**Through-line**: One core idea that everything connects to. If you can't state it in a sentence, the talk isn't focused enough.

**Sections**: Each section makes one point, supported by one or two real examples. Build toward the through-line. Signal transitions clearly - spoken language needs more signposting than written.

**Landing**: Arrive back at the through-line. Don't summarize everything you said. End with the implication or the question the talk opens up.

## Process adjustments for presentations

- **Think** - what's the one thing you want the audience to walk away with? Start here.
- **Research** - find stories, not just facts. Audiences engage with narrative. One well-told example from a real company beats five bullet points.
- **Outline** - more critical for presentations than any other medium. The structure needs to work in linear time - the audience can't jump ahead or re-read.
- **Draft** - write it more like you'd speak it. Read it aloud. If you wouldn't say it that way, rewrite it.
- **Revise** - cut aggressively. Presentations that try to cover too much cover nothing. Less material, better delivered.

## What to avoid in presentations

- Slide-driven structure. Write the talk first, make slides after. Slides support the talk, not the other way around.
- "As I mentioned earlier..." The audience either remembers or they don't. Referring back breaks flow.
- Hype language. "This is going to blow your mind" or "This changes everything." Let the content do that work.
- Performed casualness. "So, funny story..." Just tell the story. The audience will decide if it's funny.
- Dense slides with bullet points the speaker reads aloud. If it's on the slide, don't say it. If you need to say it, don't put it on the slide.

## Strongest influences for presentations

- Acquired's visible research and genuine enthusiasm
- PG's thinking-out-loud mode (adapted for spoken delivery)
- Ishiguro's pacing (let moments land, don't rush past the important parts)
