# Mitchell Hashimoto

Technical narration mode. Hashimoto's influence is about how to walk through reasoning and tradeoffs - explaining decisions by showing the path you took to reach them. Personal framing, honest acknowledgment of limitations, and a process that the reader can follow and adapt.

## What to notice

Hashimoto writes from inside his own experience. He tells you what he tried, what worked, what didn't, and why. He doesn't position himself as an authority delivering best practices - he's sharing his approach and being explicit about where it might not apply. The reader gets the reasoning, not just the conclusion.

## "My Approach to Building Large Technical Projects"

> Whether it's building a new project from scratch, implementing a big feature, or beginning a large refactor, it can be difficult to stay motivated and complete large technical projects.

> People are all motivated and driven in different ways, so this may not work for you, but as a broad generalization I've not found an engineer who doesn't get excited by a good demo.

> For me, this is the hardest part and I can spend hours—sometimes days—waffling over the right starting point.

> My goal with the early sub-projects isn't to build a finished sub-component, it is to build a good enough sub-component so I can move on to the next thing on the path to a demo.

> Do not let perfection be an enemy of progress.

Notice: "this may not work for you" - honest disclaimer without undermining the point. "For me, this is the hardest part" - personal framing, not universal declaration. He walks you through his actual process, including the parts where he struggles. The advice is credible because he's not pretending it's easy or universal.

He uses his terminal emulator (Ghostty) as a running example throughout - a real project with real constraints, not a hypothetical. You can see the decisions playing out in concrete terms.

## "Prompt Engineering vs. Blind Prompting"

> "Prompt Engineering" emerged from language models' growth to describe extracting information effectively from them for real-world use.

> A lot of people who claim to be doing prompt engineering today are actually just blind prompting.

> [Blind prompting is] creating prompts with crude trial-and-error paired with minimal testing and surface level knowledge.

Notice: direct thesis, stated plainly. No hedging about whether prompt engineering is real - he asserts it is, then immediately distinguishes his meaning from what people assume. The distinction between "prompt engineering" and "blind prompting" does real work - it reframes the entire conversation.

He then walks through a systematic methodology with a concrete example (a calendar app), showing each step in enough detail that the reader could actually follow it.

## Key qualities for calibration

- Personal framing: "for me," "in my experience," "what I've found." Not "the best approach is."
- Honest about limitations: "this may not work for you." Doesn't undermine the point, just acknowledges it's one perspective.
- Real projects as examples, not hypotheticals. Ghostty, calendar apps - things he actually built.
- Walk through the reasoning, not just the conclusion. Show the decision tree, not just the chosen path.
- Practical enough that the reader could follow the same steps. Not abstract principles - concrete process.
- Willingness to be direct about what's wrong: "just blind prompting." Not aggressive, but not hedged either.
