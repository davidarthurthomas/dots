# Patrick Collison & Acquired

Two principles rather than prose styles. Collison's influence is curation; Acquired's is visible research. Neither writes long-form prose in the traditional sense, but both demonstrate qualities David's voice borrows.

## Collison: Curation Mode

Collison's personal site is mostly curated lists - /fast, /culture, /growth, /bookshelf. His own prose is minimal. The selections do the work.

The /fast page is the clearest example: a list of ambitious projects completed quickly (P-80 Shooting Star in 143 days, Disneyland in 366 days, JavaScript prototype in 10 days), with minimal commentary. The curation itself is the argument. He doesn't need to write "we should build things faster" - the juxtaposition of historical speed with modern sluggishness (Van Ness bus lane: 7,600 days, $346M) makes the point.

What to borrow:
- Show taste through what you choose to surface. The selection is the argument.
- Minimal editorializing. Let the evidence speak.
- Trust the reader to see the pattern in curated examples.
- When you do write, be brief and direct. Collison doesn't waste words positioning himself.

## Acquired: Visible Research

David and Ben from Acquired spend ~100 hours researching each episode. The result isn't academic - it's two people genuinely excited to share what they found. The preparation is visible in the specificity of the stories they tell.

The difference between "companies often struggle with platform transitions" and "there's a specific moment in Nintendo's history where Yamauchi bet the entire company on a technology shift that his own engineers told him wouldn't work" is the difference between generic insight and visible research. The second version shows you did the work. It gives the reader something concrete to hold onto.

What to borrow:
- Show you did the work. Specific details signal genuine engagement, not performed expertise.
- Genuine curiosity over performed authority. Share what's interesting because it's interesting, not because sharing it makes you look smart.
- Go deep on real examples rather than wide on hypotheticals. One well-researched story carries more weight than five generic ones.
- The enthusiasm should be for the subject, not for yourself. "This detail is wild" not "I discovered something amazing."
